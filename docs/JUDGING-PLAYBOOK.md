# RELIQUNIX.SPACE — a stronger judging case

The project’s claim is educational: it helps school-age and casual space enthusiasts connect hardware left behind with the engineering and discoveries it made possible. It does not remove debris, predict deterioration, or measure present-day artifact condition.

| Pillar | Demonstrate in the product | Evidence the team still needs |
|---|---|---|
| Impact | The three-stop Field Mission, component text alternatives, discovery questions, printable classroom worksheet, and a learner-authored field report. | Observe real learners, collect their explanations before and after, and report the actual sample size and outcomes. Current local activity counts show participation, not validated learning gain. |
| Creativity | Move from a physical component to NASA evidence, then let the visitor become a curator whose interpretation travels home in a report. | Explain how the combination differs from a passive 3D gallery. Do not claim the concept has never been attempted. |
| Validity | 36 mission facts and 36 timeline events linked to 27 NASA resources; an exportable evidence pack; explicit model and simulation limits. | Ask a knowledgeable reviewer to check the historical wording, model adaptations, and component mappings. Keep an issue list and resolve errors. |
| Relevance | Eagle, Surveyor 3, and InSight form a focused story about return engineering, hardware as evidence, and scientific legacy. All twelve records remain available. | Confirm that learners can explain the hardware-to-science connection, rather than only name a mission. |
| Presentation | A clear first-use mission, source links beside claims, session progress, a curator story, and a report that can be printed. | Rehearse a concise live demonstration with a backup recording and a deployed URL tested on another device. |

The five pillars have equal priority in the rubric supplied for this project. This checklist is a preparation aid, not a prediction of a judging score.

## A focused 90-second pitch

**0–15 seconds — the problem.** “A mission’s story usually ends when its signal stops. But the hardware left on other worlds still holds the engineering choices and discoveries that made exploration possible. We turn those objects into a learning experience.”

**15–35 seconds — the experience.** Show the continuous descent to Eagle. Open the landing-gear or thermal-blanket component. Explain why the lower stage stayed when the crew returned to orbit.

**35–50 seconds — the evidence.** Open Surveyor 3’s mission file. Show a fact, its citation, and the later recovery of hardware by Apollo 12. Distinguish the historical record from the interpretive model.

**50–65 seconds — the discovery.** Show InSight’s seismic instrument and the connection between a surface sensor and the investigation of Mars’ interior.

**65–80 seconds — the learning outcome.** Open the Field Mission. Show its component record, discovery question, and Curator’s Desk. Select a NASA fact and write a short interpretation. Download the field report.

**80–90 seconds — the impact case.** State the real pilot result if a pilot has been conducted. Otherwise say: “The classroom activity is ready for a learner pilot; we have not yet measured educational effectiveness.” End with the intended next classroom or museum use.

This is a rehearsal format, not an assertion about an event’s required presentation length. Follow the current submission instructions for your event.

## A practical learner pilot

1. Invite 5–10 people representative of the intended audience, including at least one educator if possible. Obtain appropriate permission for any school-age participants. Avoid recording names in the project’s field reports.
2. Before showing the app, ask: “Why would a successful mission leave hardware behind?” and “How could a stationary lander investigate the inside of a planet?” Use the worksheet’s blank reflection as a baseline.
3. Let each person try the Field Mission with minimal coaching. Record completion time, navigation problems, and whether they can find a primary source.
4. Ask the same conceptual questions after the activity, plus: “Show one component and explain what science it enabled.” Use an educator-reviewed rubric to compare explanations.
5. Report observed results with sample size, age range, testing conditions, and limitations. Separate a correct answer after retries from an independently explained concept. Small convenience samples do not establish broad effectiveness.
6. Fix the most common problem, then test that change with new users. Use only real feedback and actual outcomes in the presentation.

## High-value next additions after the pilot

- An educator-reviewed Arabic edition, with proper right-to-left layouts, if the target classrooms need it.
- A museum partner’s artifact question or classroom use case, documented with permission.
- One NASA observation-versus-reconstruction comparison, with dates and exact imagery credits, to make model uncertainty easier to understand.
- A short subtitled demonstration recording for unstable venue connections.

Do not add fake user counts, fabricated testimonials, unverified “first-ever” claims, or a live AI history assistant without reliable evidence controls. More features will not substitute for a clear learning outcome and a tested story.
